# Summer Reflection: AI Evaluation

This summer, my understanding of AI evaluation grew in stages. I started by learning what evaluation systems for agents and LLMs actually are; there's real structure behind them and not just "does it look right." Testing can happen by hand through manual review, through deterministic checks with absolute pass/fail terms, or through other AI models acting as judges. From there, I learned that metrics themselves come in many forms depending on what you're trying to catch — like relevancy, correctness, hallucination, tool usage, and more.

Before any of that testing could be automated, though, I had to lay the groundwork. I wrote an Architecture Decision Record (ADR), a product brief (PRD), and a technical design document (TDD) to formalize how the evaluation system should actually be built. We used (DeepEval framework)[https://github.com/confident-ai/deepeval], which gave us built-in metrics out of the box, but also let us build our own metrics that measured response accuracy. These assessed whether the agent correctly called (or withheld) a tool at the right moment, and whether it stayed relevant to what the user actually asked. Building those custom metrics forced a much deeper understanding of Magnetchat.ai as a product: what it actually does for users, and what "correct" behavior even means in a sales conversation. That pushed me to learn sales qualification frameworks like BANT and its variants in real depth. I had to understand it not as abstract theory, but as the literal criteria our metrics had to encode.

That connection cut both ways: changing which framework we evaluated against drastically changed how the agent's responses were judged. This posed a challenge for our evaluation system, which meant our metrics couldn't stay static either and had to evolve alongside the sales logic they were meant to test. To keep track of all this as it evolved, we stored our results in an HTML results page, giving us a persistent and easy-to-read record of how the agent performed across runs rather than losing that history in terminal output.

Looking ahead, the natural next step is closing the loop on what the system has already found — feeding documented issues like CTA hesitation back into the agent's logic and re-running the suite to confirm the fix holds. From there, I'd like to wire up tool-correctness checks so those kinds of gaps get caught automatically instead of by manual review, integrate the suite into CI so evaluation runs on every change rather than on demand, and eventually expand our test coverage using synthetic case generation so we're not limited to the scenarios we can write by hand. Together, these would turn what started as a summer-long audit into lasting infrastructure the team can keep relying on.
